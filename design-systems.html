Design Systems

Two systems, two different contexts: one built from scratch inside a startup's product team, the other developed for a consultancy and reused across multiple client engagements. Here's how I think about the difference.

Scope3 products
TypeIn-house · Reused & adapted
FocusConsistency, dev speed, UI/UX debt reduction

Why This Matters

A design system isn't just a component library — it's a bet about how a product or organization will grow. The right bet depends entirely on context. A startup building its first real product needs something different from a consultancy serving multiple clients under one roof. I've built for both, and the two experiences taught me different lessons.

System 1 — Built From Scratch (Startup Context)

At Lynit, a startup, there was no existing component library to inherit — and no other team or client to coordinate around. That's the defining feature of this kind of system: it's built for one product's long-term evolution, with the freedom (and pressure) of starting from a blank page.

I designed the system using an atomic approach — tokens, base components, and composition patterns for a product still finding its shape. Just as important: I brought a junior designer onto the system and mentored them into owning its maintenance. A system only stays alive if someone other than its original architect can run it.

System 2 — Reused Across Client Engagements (Consultancy Context)

Polistat and Mapeo de Decisiones were two separate client projects delivered by the same organization — a consultancy, not a single product team. That changes the design-system question entirely: instead of "how does this system grow with one product," the question becomes "how does this system survive being handed to a different client with different branding and different constraints."

Rather than building Mapeo's system from zero, I evolved Polistat's component library into its foundation — rebranded for the new client, and migrated the working files from Adobe XD to Sketch in the process. Less visible than a from-scratch build. Considerably more valuable at the organizational level, because it meant the second engagement didn't cost as much as the first.

What Each Approach Actually Solves

  • Consistency across engagements — for the consultancy work specifically, a shared, adaptable system meant Mapeo didn't start from a blank slate or drift into inconsistent patterns against Polistat
  • Development speed — in both contexts, a documented system meant engineering wasn't re-deciding the same UI questions on every new feature or client engagement
  • Reduced UI/UX debt — governance (in the startup case) and disciplined reuse (in the consultancy case) both prevent the same failure mode: one-off components accumulating until nobody can say what the "real" pattern is anymore

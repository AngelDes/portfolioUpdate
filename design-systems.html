Design Systems

Two systems, two very different contexts — one built from scratch inside a startup's product team, the other developed for a consultancy and reused across multiple client engagements under the same organization.

Scope3 products
TypeIn-house · Reused & adapted

Why This Matters

A design system isn't just a component library — it's a bet about how a product or organization will grow. I've built both kinds, in two very different settings: a system designed from zero for one startup's product, and a system designed for a consultancy, where it had to serve multiple separate client engagements rather than a single product roadmap.

System 1 — Lynit: Built In-House, From Scratch (Startup)

Lynit was a startup, and its own product — there was no existing component library to inherit, and no other team or client to coordinate with. I designed the system from the ground up using an atomic approach, defining tokens, base components, and composition patterns for a product still finding its shape.

I didn't maintain it alone. I brought a junior designer onto the system and mentored them through ownership of it — part of building not just a system, but a team capable of sustaining one.

System 2 — Polistat & Mapeo de Decisiones: Shared Across Client Engagements

Polistat and Mapeo de Decisiones were two separate client projects delivered by the same organization — not a single product, but a consultancy serving multiple clients. Rather than building Mapeo's system from zero, I evolved Polistat's component library into its foundation, rebranding it for the new client and migrating the working files from Adobe XD to Sketch along the way.

This is the harder, less glamorous side of systems work: making a system portable enough to survive a rebrand and a tooling migration without losing consistency or quality along the way.

2Client projects sharing one system
1Full rebrand executed on the shared system
XD → SketchTooling migration handled alongside reuse

What It Solved

  • Consistency across engagements — Polistat and Mapeo were different clients served by the same organization; a shared, adaptable system meant each engagement didn't start from a blank slate or drift into inconsistent patterns
  • Development speed — shared, documented components meant engineering wasn't rebuilding the same UI decisions on every new feature or client engagement
  • Reduced UI/UX debt — a governed system meant fewer one-off components accumulating over time, and a clearer path to fixing inconsistencies at the source rather than screen by screen

Lynit

A tool for writers to outline complex stories — and the project where I learned what it actually takes to build a design system and a design team from nothing.

RoleSr. UX Designer, Team Lead
Duration2 years
MethodologiesDesign Thinking, Shape Up, Atomic Design
ToolsFigma, Principle, Miro, Amplitude, LogRocket

About

Lynit helps writers outline their stories using data visualization and mind mapping — a visual way to see how story elements connect, instead of losing the thread in pages of notes.

We launched v1 and grew from 0 to around 500 users in a couple of months, which felt like validation. It wasn't, not entirely. Underneath the growth number was a retention problem: new users weren't coming back, and roughly 60% never even discovered the product's core connection features. Good acquisition, leaky product.

My Contribution

I owned the redesign end to end, which at a startup means the boundaries of "design" get blurry fast. I wasn't just designing screens — I was building the process that would let us keep designing well as we grew.

  • Led problem definition and prioritization using the Shape Up framework
  • Directed research: journey mapping, funnel analysis, user and stakeholder interviews
  • Defined and governed the product's design principles and design system
  • Led ideation, wireframing, prototyping, and usability testing
  • Built the measurement framework: baselines, dashboards, design-impact reporting
  • Led and mentored the design team; managed dev collaboration and phased launch

The Problem

Only 10% of new users ever updated a connection, and just 17% of active subscribers came back after their first week. Journey mapping made the reasons obvious once we actually looked: connection creation was too slow, users didn't understand why it mattered, and the first screen threw everything at them at once with no clear place to start.

Problem Definition with Shape Up

I'd worked in enough loosely-scoped sprints to know how design work quietly expands to fill however much time you give it. I pushed for Shape Up specifically to put a hard edge on that:

  • Used "appetite" to cap time investment per problem — fixed 6-week cycles instead of open-ended scoping
  • Ran structured "shaping" sessions with stakeholders to define problems tightly before design work began
  • Wrote pitch docs stating the problem, constraints, and solution boundaries up front
  • Organized small, autonomous teams around well-defined problems
  • Used hill charts to track progress and surface risk early

What changed wasn't just process — it was the conversation. The team stopped reacting to feature requests and started arguing about problems, which is a much better argument to be having.

Design Principles & Design System

There was no existing system to inherit — I built it from zero, which is either a gift or a burden depending on the week. I pushed for the investment in an atomic system even when the timeline argued against it, and had to make the case for why slowing down now would speed us up later.

Principles

  • Clarity Over Complexity — clear mental models over feature abundance
  • Progressive Disclosure — reveal functionality as users gain expertise
  • Visual Storytelling — use visualization to reveal narrative structure
  • Friction-Free Creation — minimize barriers to capturing ideas
  • Coherent Ecosystem — all parts of the system work together

These principles ended up doing more work than I expected — not just guiding design decisions, but settling debates and giving new team members something solid to stand on from day one.

Leadership & Stakeholder Management

  • Acted as the primary bridge between engineering, executives, and the design team; negotiated shared success metrics with engineering leadership
  • Instituted weekly cross-functional design reviews and pair-design sessions with frontend developers, cutting design-clarification requests by roughly 40%
  • Presented progress and impact metrics to executives to secure buy-in and resources for post-launch iteration
  • Brought a junior designer onto the design system and mentored them into owning its maintenance

Data & Measurement Approach

  • Established pre-redesign baselines for credible before/after comparison
  • Partnered with data/analytics to build custom Amplitude dashboards tied to specific design changes
  • Introduced recurring design-impact reports connecting UX changes to business metrics, reviewed at the leadership level
  • Tracked connection creation/update rate, weekly return rate, time-to-first-connection, session depth, and support tickets tied to navigation

Results & Impact

+30%Returning users after launch
+40%Connection creation rate
-25%Time-to-first-connection
-35%Feature implementation time

Qualitative

  • The team adopted shared design principles that resolved debates and sped up decisions
  • Design-impact reporting became the standard used to evaluate every subsequent release
  • Engineering reported far fewer design-clarification requests after the system rollout

Looking Back

Lynit is where I actually learned what "design system" means beyond components in a file — it's a bet on how a team will work together months from now, made under a deadline that doesn't care if you're right yet. It's also where I learned that mentoring someone into owning that system matters as much as building it. The system outlasted me on the project; the person I trained to run it didn't need me to.

Business Goals

The redesign directly addressed Lynit's core retention problem, turning a leaky onboarding funnel into a repeatable, measured process — and gave the organization a design system and measurement framework built to support future release cycles.
